## Introduction

The rapid proliferation of electronic wallets (e-wallets) in the Philippines represents one of the most significant shifts in the country's financial landscape over the past decade. Platforms such as GCash, Maya (formerly PayMaya), ShopeePay, and the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas (BSP)-mandated QR Ph system have fundamentally altered how millions of Filipinos transact — accelerating a transition from a cash-heavy economy toward interoperable digital payment infrastructure. The COVID-19 pandemic served as a critical inflection point: BSP data from its 2021 Financial Inclusion Survey recorded a doubling of e-money account ownership from 2019 to 2021, driven in part by government-to-person (G2P) cash transfer programs and the practical necessity of contactless commerce during lockdowns.

For Davao City — the economic hub of Mindanao and one of the Philippines' largest cities by land area — digital payment adoption carries particular significance. As a regional center for commerce, agriculture-based trade (cacao, banana, durian supply chains), and a growing Information Technology-Business Process Management (IT-BPM) sector, Davao's MSME ecosystem and consumer base represent a microcosm of the broader national financial inclusion challenge. The city's mix of formally banked urban professionals, semi-banked market vendors in Bankerohan and Agdao, and unbanked agricultural workers in peripheral barangays creates a layered adoption landscape that national-level data alone cannot fully illuminate.

Artificial intelligence tools — including large language model (LLM)-based research assistants — offer researchers genuine utility in synthesizing the rapidly expanding literature on digital finance. AI can aggregate findings across multiple publications, identify thematic patterns, and produce structured summaries in a fraction of the time required for manual review. However, this efficiency comes with a critical and well-documented risk: AI systems can generate plausible-sounding but factually incorrect statements, fabricate citations, misattribute statistics, and reproduce systemic biases embedded in their training data — including an over-representation of Metro Manila and Global North fintech contexts at the expense of regional Philippine perspectives.

This portfolio project demonstrates a complete audit cycle: an AI-generated literature review on e-wallet adoption in Davao City, followed by rigorous human verification. The audit exposes hallucinations, unsupported claims, and regional biases while modeling the verification protocols that responsible academic research workflows must employ.

### Financial Inclusion and the Philippine Digital Payment Landscape

Financial inclusion has emerged as a central policy objective of the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, anchored by the National Strategy for Financial Inclusion (NSFI) 2022–2028 and the Digital Payments Transformation Roadmap (DPTR) 2020–2023. The DPTR set an ambitious target of converting 50% of the total volume of retail payment transactions to digital form by 2023, alongside the goal of ensuring that 70% of Filipino adults would have transaction accounts by the same year (BSP, 2020). These targets reflected a recognition that the Philippines — despite high mobile phone penetration — remained among the more cash-dependent economies in Southeast Asia.

The BSP's 2021 Financial Inclusion Survey (FIS) documented notable progress: e-money account ownership among Filipino adults rose from approximately 8% in 2019 to 36% in 2021 `[?]`, a shift widely attributed to the combined effect of pandemic-driven digitization and the expansion of GCash and Maya's agent networks into previously underserved municipalities. GCash, operated by Mynt (a Globe Telecom affiliate), reportedly surpassed 60 million registered users by the end of 2022, making it one of the largest financial service providers in the Philippines by user count (Mynt, 2022) `[?]`.

### GCash and Maya: Platform Adoption Dynamics

The duopolistic structure of the Philippine e-wallet market — dominated by GCash and Maya — has been extensively studied in the context of Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) frameworks. Research by Reyes and Santos (2021) published in the *Asian Journal of Finance and Accounting* found that perceived ease of use, social influence, and mobile internet access were the strongest predictors of GCash adoption among urban Filipino millennials `[?]`. Crucially, the study noted that trust — operationalized as confidence in platform security and reliability of fund transfers — was a more significant adoption barrier than digital literacy for users in Visayas and Mindanao regions compared to Metro Manila respondents.

Maya, formerly PayMaya and now operating as a digital bank following BSP's grant of a digital banking license in 2021, has positioned itself distinctly from GCash by targeting the banked and semi-banked segment through its Maya Bank savings product, which offered a 6% per annum interest rate on deposits at launch `[?]`. The competitive dynamics between these two platforms have driven rapid feature expansion — including buy-now-pay-later (BNPL) products, micro-insurance integrations, and merchant QR code proliferation — that collectively accelerated MSME adoption beyond early projections.

### MSME Digital Payment Adoption

Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises constitute approximately 99.5% of all business establishments in the Philippines, accounting for 62.4% of total employment, according to the 2022 MSME Statistics report published by the Department of Trade and Industry (DTI, 2022) `[?]`. Despite this economic centrality, MSME digital payment adoption has been characterized by significant heterogeneity. A DTI-BSP joint study conducted in 2021 found that only 23% of MSMEs in Mindanao had integrated any form of digital payment acceptance `[?]`, compared to 41% in the National Capital Region — a gap attributable to infrastructure limitations, lower formal banking penetration, and the cash-preference norms of regional market economies.

In Davao City specifically, the Davao City Investment Promotion Center reported that MSME digital payment adoption accelerated significantly between 2020 and 2022, with QR Ph merchant registrations in Bankerohan Market increasing by 340% over the two-year period `[?]`. This growth was supported in part by the Davao City government's *Negosyo Center* partnership with BSP's financial literacy programs, which conducted over 120 onboarding workshops for market vendors in Districts 1 through 11 `[?]`.

### Consumer Trust, Security Concerns, and Barriers to Adoption

Consumer trust remains a persistent and well-documented barrier to sustained e-wallet adoption across the Philippines. Velasco (2020), writing in the *Philippine Journal of Development*, identified four primary trust dimensions relevant to Filipino e-wallet users: institutional trust in the platform operator, system trust in the underlying technology infrastructure, interpersonal trust derived from peer usage norms, and regulatory trust in BSP's oversight capacity `[?]`. The study found that rural and peri-urban respondents — categories that include many residents of Davao City's outer barangays such as Marilog and Calinan — scored significantly lower on institutional and system trust dimensions, reflecting both limited prior banking experience and higher reported incidence of phishing and SIM-swap fraud in these communities.

The BSP's Anti-Financial Crime and Consumer Protection framework, strengthened through BSP Circular 1140 (2022) on cybersecurity governance for BSP-supervised financial institutions, has sought to address these structural trust deficits. However, the circular's implementation requirements apply primarily to formally supervised entities, leaving informal digital wallet agents in peripheral areas outside its direct regulatory perimeter.

### Digital Finance in the Davao Region Context

Davao City's positioning as Mindanao's premier economic hub creates both opportunities and tensions in the digital finance adoption narrative. The city's IT-BPM sector — employing an estimated 45,000 workers as of 2022 `[?]` — represents a high-adoption demographic that skews regional e-wallet statistics positively. Simultaneously, the city's substantial agricultural hinterland and large informal market sector create pockets of persistent under-adoption that aggregate city-level data can obscure.

The convergence of BSP's DPTR objectives, DTI's MSME digitalization agenda, and local government initiatives such as the Davao City Digital Transformation Roadmap positions the city as a strategically important site for studying differentiated adoption within a single administrative unit. Future research employing disaggregated barangay-level data — potentially drawing on PSA's Community-Based Monitoring System (CBMS) datasets — would materially advance the field's understanding of the within-city heterogeneity that current literature inadequately captures.

## 4. Critical Reflection on AI Limitations

### Strengths of AI in Literature Synthesis

Large language models offer meaningful practical value in the early stages of academic literature review. Their capacity to rapidly synthesize thematic patterns across a broad conceptual landscape — in this case, spanning financial inclusion theory, platform adoption models, regulatory frameworks, and regional economic development — enables researchers to quickly construct a working conceptual scaffold. The AI-generated review in this project correctly identified the BSP Digital Payments Transformation Roadmap as a central policy anchor, accurately represented the competitive dynamics between GCash and Maya, and appropriately framed consumer trust and infrastructure limitations as adoption barriers. These thematic orientations reflect genuine patterns in the literature and provide a useful departure point for structured manual searching.

AI tools also demonstrate value in identifying relevant theoretical frameworks (such as the Technology Acceptance Model) and connecting empirical phenomena to established conceptual models — a task that can require significant reading time when done manually, particularly for researchers entering an unfamiliar interdisciplinary domain.

### Weaknesses and the Fabrication Problem

The audit conducted in this project revealed a 30% outright hallucination rate across sampled statements — a finding consistent with documented research on LLM accuracy in academic contexts (Alkaissi & McFarlane, 2023). The pattern of fabrication observed here is particularly instructive: AI-generated hallucinations were not random or obviously incorrect. They were structurally plausible, locally inflected, and statistically precise. The fabricated DCIPC Bankerohan Market statistic (340% QR Ph growth) is more dangerous than a factual error precisely because its specificity mimics the appearance of primary research. A researcher under time pressure who does not manually trace every citation and statistic to its source would have no surface-level reason to doubt it.

The citation hallucination problem — generating nonexistent journal articles with plausible author names, publication years, and journal titles — represents a direct threat to the integrity of academic literature. Unlike a misattributed but real finding, a phantom citation cannot be traced, rebutted, or corrected within the existing scholarly record. Once such a citation enters a published paper, it may propagate through subsequent literature reviews that themselves rely partially on AI assistance.

### The Regional Bias Problem

Beyond factual fabrication, this audit identified a structural bias problem that is arguably more pervasive and harder to detect: the systematic under-representation of regional Philippine perspectives in AI-synthesized content. The AI defaulted to national-level BSP statistics when city-specific data should have been foregrounded, produced conceptual frameworks rooted in English-language fintech literature rather than locally grounded studies from Davao institutions, and failed to acknowledge the significant body of unpublished or grey literature — DTI-XI field reports, LGU assessments, Negosyo Center program evaluations — that constitutes much of the empirically grounded knowledge base for regional MSME research in Mindanao.

### Lessons from the Audit

The audit process demonstrated that responsible AI-assisted research requires a verification discipline that is more demanding, not less, than traditional literature review. Because AI outputs are fluent, well-structured, and plausibly cited, they create a cognitive condition in which errors are easier to miss than in obviously rough or incomplete research drafts. Researchers must approach AI-generated literature reviews with active skepticism — treating every statistic as unverified until traced to a primary source, and every citation as potentially fabricated until physically located in a library database or official publication portal.

## 5. Recommendations for Future Research Workflows

**1. Implement Mandatory Citation Triangulation**
Every citation generated by an AI tool must be independently located through at least two separate verification pathways — a library database search (Google Scholar, Scopus, JSTOR, or the Philippine e-Journals portal) and a direct institutional source check (BSP's website, DTI's official publications portal, PSA's OpenSTAT). Citations that cannot be located through both pathways should be removed and replaced with a documented research gap note, not retained on the assumption that the source may exist somewhere.

**2. Establish a Local Source Audit Checklist**
Research on Davao City and Mindanao requires a localization verification layer that national databases cannot supply. Before finalizing any literature review, researchers should check: (a) DTI-XI regional publications and press releases; (b) BSP regional office reports for the Davao Region; (c) thesis repositories at ADDU, UP Mindanao, USP-Davao, and DLSU-Dasmariñas; (d) Davao City LGU official documents; and (e) PIDS regional research outputs. AI tools have low recall for these sources, making human search essential.

**3. Adopt a Confidence Tagging Protocol During AI Generation**
When using AI to draft literature summaries, instruct the model to tag every statistical claim with a confidence indicator and a source type (e.g., `[STAT — requires BSP FIS citation]` or `[CITE — author/year plausible, not verified]`). This practice, demonstrated in Section 1 of this project through `[?]` flags, converts the AI output from a finished draft into an annotated working document — psychologically reinforcing the researcher's obligation to verify rather than accept.

**4. Maintain a Hallucination and Bias Registry for the Research Project**
Rather than treating discovered errors as isolated corrections, researchers should maintain a running log of every AI-generated error encountered during a project — as demonstrated in Section 3 of this portfolio. This registry serves three functions: it creates an audit trail demonstrating research rigor; it surfaces systematic patterns (e.g., consistent over-reliance on Metro Manila data) that indicate structural AI biases requiring compensatory manual research; and it provides a basis for transparent methods reporting in the final publication, in line with emerging academic norms on AI use disclosure.

**5. Document AI Tool Use Transparently in Methodology Sections**
Published research that employed AI assistance at any stage of the literature review process should disclose this in the methodology section, specifying: the AI tool and version used; the prompt structure employed; the extent of AI-generated content in the final literature review; and the verification protocols applied. This practice aligns with the emerging policies of journals such as *Nature*, *PLOS ONE*, and Philippine academic outlets, and is a professional responsibility that protects the researcher, the institution, and the integrity of the scholarly record.
